## Facebook — a practical question

You corrected a chapter. Does its narration still teach the old version?

Our AI Jarvis workforce production pilot explores a small but useful habit: record which source version each adaptation uses. Then, when the source changes, inspect the work that depends on it.

The lesson includes a fictional studio team, a worked assignment, and a practice exercise. Its aim is to help a person explain what was done, what was checked, and what decision comes next.

Try the question on one project today: could another person identify the version you actually reviewed?

## LinkedIn — an operational lesson

An AI-assisted workflow becomes easier to manage when the next person can inspect a concrete result.

For a publishing assignment, that means more than asking for a chapter, an audiobook, and a video. It means naming the audience, the source version, the acceptance criteria, and the review that is still needed.

The AI Jarvis workforce pilot uses one chapter and three text adaptations to make that relationship visible. A change in the chapter can affect a narration script. A change in narration can affect a storyboard. Recording those dependencies helps a reviewer ask the right question about the right version.

The teaching example keeps source consistency, editorial acceptance, recorded media, and publication as separate milestones. That gives the person directing the work a clearer decision at each stage.

A useful team exercise: choose one recurring assignment and write a definition of done that another person could reasonably reject.

## Threads — four-post teaching sequence

**Post 1:** A file can look current while using an old source. This becomes easy to miss when one chapter turns into narration, short videos, and a storyboard.

**Post 2:** Give each adaptation a record of the exact source version it used. Then a change becomes a question you can investigate: which dependent work needs review?

**Post 3:** Updating a recorded hash does not rewrite a sentence or accept an explanation. Inspect the affected content before marking a new parent version.

**Post 4:** Try it with a small assignment. Name the audience, deliverable, acceptance criteria, source, and next human decision. Keep the exercise's evidence with the result.

## SubStack — opening essay and proposed title

**Title:** Give the next person a result they can review

The hardest part of an AI-assisted publishing assignment may be deciding what counts as finished.

A chapter can exist while its exercise is unclear. A narration script can be polished while repeating an outdated sentence. A storyboard can be complete while the recording it describes has never been made. The person coordinating the work needs a way to see those distinctions.

The AI Jarvis workforce production pilot begins with one small assignment: help a studio operator define a result that another person can accept. Its fictional team prepares a chapter and three adaptations. Then a source changes, and the team has to explain which evidence is now out of date.

This is a useful place to practice coordination because the work stays inspectable. The reader can name the source, see the dependency, and explain why a new version needs attention. A hash helps identify a file, while editorial review answers whether its explanation remains accurate and useful.

The exercise also asks the reader to count the work. A hypothetical sales scenario can show how costs interact, but it cannot establish demand. The cost of review and correction belongs in the production picture alongside the time spent drafting.

The question for the next assignment is simple: what would another person need to see before accepting this version for its intended use?
