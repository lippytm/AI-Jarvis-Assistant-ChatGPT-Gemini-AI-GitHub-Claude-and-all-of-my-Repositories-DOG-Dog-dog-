# Narration: Give the next person a result they can review

AI Jarvis and the Empowered Human Workforce — Chapter 6 companion.

Status: original AI-assisted narration draft adapted from the accompanying chapter. No audio has been recorded. This is an abridgment, not an unabridged audiobook. The fictional team remains fictional. Intended treatment: a calm explanatory voice with short thinking pauses. Select and document the actual narrator or permitted voice before production; no person's voice is specified for imitation.

Planned listening duration: roughly 7–9 minutes, subject to a recorded read-through, pacing, and pauses. Bracketed directions and section titles are production notes and are not spoken.

## N1 — The assignment that nobody could accept

[Brief pause before opening.]

Imagine a small training studio preparing a lesson about AI assistants. Nadia directs the project. Leo creates drafts. Inez checks the explanation. These people are invented for this lesson.

Nadia begins with a broad request: make something impressive about AI assistants.

Leo returns with twenty titles and several energetic slogans. There is plenty of material, but the next person cannot decide whether the assignment is complete. Nadia has not chosen the reader's outcome. Inez cannot tell which claims need evidence.

Nadia tries again. Help a first-time studio operator prepare one reviewable assignment. Teach the reader to name the output, identify its source version, and explain what to do when that source changes. Use fictional examples and local text files. Finish with an exercise that needs no paid AI account.

Now the team has a result it can inspect. The larger book can grow from a series of assignments like this one.

## N2 — A file and an outcome are different evidence

The requested files are a chapter, a narration script, a short-video script, and a video-book storyboard. The desired outcome is a person's ability to direct the work and recognize an outdated adaptation.

A file check can show that the four text files exist. A content check can show that a file matches a recorded version. An editor can judge the clarity of the explanation. A learner exercise can show whether a reader understands it.

Each observation answers a different question. A script is not a recording. A verified packet is not a published product. A completed lesson file is not proof that somebody learned the lesson.

[Pause for the listener to consider a recent assignment.]

Think about a task you delegated. What artifact did you ask for? What did a person need to be able to do afterward? Write those as separate sentences.

## N3 — A small contract for useful work

Before multiplying agents, Nadia records the assignment. The reader is a small studio operator or educator. The outcome is to prepare a bounded brief and find an adaptation affected by a source change.

The inputs are original fictional examples and named local draft files. The first pass produces text. It does not record media, change accounts, or create a sales offer.

The definition of done is concrete: the four sources are present, their declared versions match, the dependency relationships are consistent, and unfinished reviews are visible.

The human editor's later decision is also clear: is this specific version useful enough for a reader trial?

This contract makes a creative suggestion easier to handle. An agent can propose animation or a longer workshop. The suggestion becomes something to assess, instead of silently changing the current assignment.

## N4 — Three distinct questions

The coordinator asks whether the packet preserves the objective, exact sources, blockers, and next action.

The adaptation producer asks how the lesson should change for a listener or viewer. A spoken explanation may need a pause where the chapter uses a table. A short video may focus on only one decision.

The challenger asks what could cause a reader to believe more than the evidence supports. Did the headline turn a proposal into a promise? Did the shortened version remove an important condition?

These are proposed roles. Naming ChatGPT Business, Gemini AI, or Claude as intended workspaces does not establish that they are connected. The local project can prepare their handoff briefs without sending them.

The human editor retains the next decision. A review of a chapter does not automatically accept a separate recording, website page, or commercial release.

## N5 — Follow one changed sentence

Leo finishes the chapter and adapts it into narration. The narration records the exact chapter version it used. The short-video script does the same. The video-book storyboard records both the chapter and the narration.

Then Inez spots a problem. One sentence says that a verified packet is ready to sell. The team changes the chapter to explain that verification covers local consistency, while creative review and release remain separate.

The narration still contains the old sentence.

If the team remembers only filenames, both drafts may look current. If the adaptation records its parent version, the mismatch becomes visible.

The first repair is to record the changed chapter correctly. The second repair is to inspect the adaptations. Updating a source hash does not edit the narration, judge the new explanation, or prove that a reviewer accepted it.

A content hash helps identify particular bytes. It does not prove that the statement is true, establish rights to use it, or authenticate a reviewer's decision.

The team keeps the failed packet. It now has a clear example of what changed, which check noticed it, and why the new adaptation was needed.

## N6 — Ask what each format contributes

The chapter carries the full reasoning. The narration offers a sequence a listener can follow. The short video demonstrates the changed-source problem. The video book adds visual decisions and a practice pause.

The wording can vary while the lesson stays accountable to its source. Record what was shortened, omitted, or added. If an adaptation adds a new claim, identify what evidence it needs.

Plan for the actual audience. Explain important visual information aloud. Avoid making color the only way to recognize a problem. Check captions against the eventual recording. A plan for accessibility still needs review of the finished media.

## N7 — Count the work before making a business claim

Consider a hypothetical future edited digital edition priced at nine dollars. Assume sixty dollars of fixed cash cost, four hours of production valued at twenty dollars an hour, fifty cents of delivery cost per unit, and a five-percent variable fee assumption.

Under those assumptions, each unit contributes eight dollars and five cents before fixed costs. Eight units cover the assumed fixed cash cost. Eighteen cover that cost and the assumed value of production time.

These are arithmetic examples, not observed sales or provider prices. Taxes, refunds, customer acquisition, ongoing support, and other costs may change the result. The public teaching example is not a checkout offer.

The useful question is which assumption needs evidence next. If readers cannot complete the exercise, improve the teaching before treating a sales scenario as proof of demand.

## N8 — Your turn

[Give the listener time to pause the recording.]

Take one small assignment. Name the person who needs it, the artifact they will receive, and the outcome they should demonstrate. Identify the exact sources. Say what a reviewer could reject.

Then change one source. Explain which adaptation needs attention and which evidence is now out of date. Propose a recovery that preserves the original result.

Your acceptance note should name the version, the checks you performed, what remains untested, and the next human decision.

That is a practical contribution a coordinator can make: help a person see the goal, the work, and the evidence clearly enough to decide what happens next.
